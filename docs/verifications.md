## 1. W(Ã₋ₙ) ≅ W(Dₙ₊₁) for every even n

Let n be even and B = B(Ã₋ₙ), the odd cycle on n + 1 negative nodes. Since
B(eᵢ, eᵢ) = −2, the generators are sᵢ(v) = v − Q(v, eᵢ) eᵢ with Q = −B, which is
positive definite (Remark 3.26). In ℝⁿ⁺¹ with the dot product put

* rᵢ = (−1)ⁱ (εᵢ₊₁ − εᵢ₊₂) for 0 ≤ i ≤ n − 1, and
* rₙ = ε₁ + εₙ₊₁.

These are roots of Dₙ₊₁, and their Gram matrix is exactly Q: consecutive rᵢ have dot
product +1, rₙ meets r₀ and rₙ₋₁ with +1 (this uses n even), and all other pairs are
orthogonal. So the integer matrix T = [r₀ | … | rₙ] has Tᵀ T = Q and det T = ±2, and
T sᵢ T⁻¹ is the orthogonal reflection in rᵢ. Conjugation by T is therefore an injective
homomorphism from W(Ã₋ₙ) into O(n + 1).

Its image is generated by reflections in roots of Dₙ₊₁, so it lies in W(Dₙ₊₁). The orbit
of {rᵢ} under these reflections is the whole Dₙ₊₁ root system, so the image contains the
reflection in every Dₙ₊₁ root, in particular in each standard simple root. Hence
w ↦ T w T⁻¹ is an isomorphism W(Ã₋ₙ) → W(Dₙ₊₁).

The test checks every step exactly (Gram matrix, T sᵢ = Rᵢ T, the root orbit, and the
simple roots) for n = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10; `verify_a_tilde_minus_isomorphism(n)` works for any
even n. For odd n the cycle has an even number of nodes, so it is bipartite and by
Theorem 1.4 W(Ã₋ₙ) is conjugate to the affine group W(Ãₙ), which is infinite. So:

> **W(Ã₋ₙ) is finite if and only if n is even, and then W(Ã₋ₙ) ≅ W(Dₙ₊₁), of order
> 2ⁿ (n + 1)!.**

## 2. Appendix D in Python

* all 16 relators hold on the matrices;
* coset enumeration (SymPy's Todd–Coxeter) gives index 16 for ⟨f₁, f₂, f₃, f₄⟩. That
  subgroup satisfies the Coxeter relations of the path A₄, so its order is at most 120,

infinite, so the 16th relator (length 16) is what makes the group finite. The

## 3. Finite mixed-sign groups (Remark A.3)

**Reduction.** If Γ is connected and W(Γ) is finite, then B(Γ) is definite.

*Proof.* W preserves a positive definite form, so V = ℝⁿ is completely reducible. Each
sᵢ has a one-dimensional (−1)-eigenspace, spanned by eᵢ, so in any W-stable splitting
V = U₁ ⊕ U₂ the vector eᵢ lies in one summand and sᵢ is the identity on the other. If
eᵢ ∈ U₁, eⱼ ∈ U₂ and B(eᵢ, eⱼ) ≠ 0, then sᵢ eⱼ = eⱼ − c eᵢ with c ≠ 0 is not in U₂,
which is a contradiction. Since Γ is connected, every eᵢ lies in the same summand, and
the eᵢ span V, so V is irreducible. By Schur's lemma the W-invariant symmetric forms on V
are the multiples of the invariant inner product, and B ≠ 0, so B is definite. ∎

This recovers Corollary 3.18 of the thesis (a connected graph with both a positive and a negative node has W(Γ) infinite), since such a B has diagonal entries +2 and −2. It also rules out every all-negative graph whose −B is not positive definite.
All-positive graphs are the classical ones (Table A.1). An all-negative graph has
W(B) = W(−B). If it is bipartite, Theorem 1.4 makes it conjugate to a Table A.1 graph,
which is Table A.2. New finite groups can only come from **all-negative graphs with an
odd cycle**.

**Search.** Finite irreducible reflection groups of rank 3 have order at most 120 and of
rank 4 at most 14400, and every product of two of their reflections has order at most 5.
So every connected graph of rank 3 or 4 with edge weights m ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6} was
enumerated up to relabelling (192 graphs at rank 3 and 11,522 at rank 4). The group of
each definite one was enumerated breadth-first; passing the bound proves the group
infinite. The type is read off from (rank, |W|, number of reflections). At rank 3 the
indefinite graphs were also enumerated, and none is finite, which confirms the reduction.
No finite graph uses weight 6.

## 4. Question 3.35: which principal elements minimize the spectral radius?

**Setup.** Principal elements with the same acyclic orientation of Γ are equal, and
trees have only one conjugacy class (Theorem 2.58), so the search runs over connected
graphs with a cycle and over orientations. All edge weights are 3, so every principal
element is an integer matrix. Its characteristic polynomial is computed exactly and the
radius is taken from the roots of its square-free factors. Graphs are taken up to
automorphism.

| Nodes | Graphs | Radius varies | Bipartite & varies | Bipartite not minimal | …and it is the maximum | Mixed & varies | Sign-sorted reaches min |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 32 | 12 | 4 | 1 | 1 | 11 | 9 |
| 5 | 300 | 194 | 36 | 8 | 8 | 180 | 153 |
| 6 | 3,694 | 3,123 | 297 | 87 | 63 | 2,992 | 2,457 |

Here *sign-sorted* means every edge between a positive and a negative node points the
same way (all positive-to-negative, or all negative-to-positive), the pattern of the last
row of Table 3.1.

**Findings.**

* **McMullen's theorem survives without mixed signs.** Every graph where the bipartite
  element fails to minimize has both signs.
* **The smallest analogue of Example 3.33 is the 4-cycle with alternating signs**
  r₁(+) – r₂(−) – r₃(+) – r₄(−) – r₁. There are two conjugacy classes. The bipartite
  element s₁s₃s₂s₄ (here also the (positive)(negative) element) has radius
  3 + 2√2 ≈ 5.82843, and the circuit s₁s₂s₃s₄ has radius ≈ 3.54646. So the bipartite
  element is the maximum, and the smallest counterexample has 4 nodes rather than 6.
* **In Example 3.33 itself** there are 98 orientation classes. The minimum over all of
  them is 3.05129, attained by s₁s₂s₃s₄s₅s₆, the first row of Table 3.1, and the maximum
  is 7.14809, attained by the (positive)(negative) row. So Table 3.1 happens to list
  both extremes.
* **Bipartite failures are usually maximal.** In all 9 failures up to 5 nodes, and 63
  of 87 at 6 nodes, the bipartite element has the largest radius of all.
* **Sign-sorting is not the answer either.** It reaches the minimum in 2,619 of 3,183
  varying mixed graphs, but fails on, for example, the alternating 4-cycle, where it
  coincides with the bipartite element.
* **On cycles the minimizing circulation depends on the sign pattern.** For a k-cycle,
  conjugacy classes are labelled by the circulation c (edges forwards minus backwards).
  `cycle_radius_by_circulation` tabulates this. For example, on the 6-cycle
  (−, +, −, +, +, +) the radii for |c| = 0, 2, 4 are 4.331, 5.038 and 3.276, and on
  (−, −, +, +, +, +) they are 2.423, 1.000 and 2.133, so neither the balanced (bipartite)
  nor the most unbalanced orientation is always best.

**Status of Question 3.35.** These data rule out the two natural answers (the bipartite
type, and the sign-sorted type) and give a 4-node counterexample to the bipartite type.
They do not suggest a simple replacement; the question stays open.
